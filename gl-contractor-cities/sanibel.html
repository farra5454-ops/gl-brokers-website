Why Building on Sanibel Is Its Own Category of Risk

Sanibel is a barrier island that has deliberately constrained its own development for decades. The city operates under a comprehensive land use plan that limits density, protects native vegetation aggressively, and imposes building standards stricter than what applies elsewhere in Lee County. For contractors, this means the regulatory dimension of a job is not a formality — permitting, vegetation review, and inspection can shape scope in ways that create genuine friction with owners when the project changes mid-course.

The vegetation ordinances deserve specific attention. Sanibel restricts the removal and alteration of native vegetation to a degree most mainland contractors do not anticipate, and the island’s conservation ethic is backed by real enforcement. A tree crew that removes protected material, or that damages vegetation on an adjoining property while working, faces both a regulatory problem and a third-party claim. General liability responds to the damage to the neighbor’s property; it does not resolve the code violation, which is why local knowledge matters as much as coverage here.

Access shapes everything else. Sanibel connects to the mainland by a single causeway, which Hurricane Ian severed in September 2022 before it was rebuilt and reopened. Every crew, delivery, dumpster, and piece of equipment moves across that one route. The practical effects are constant — narrow delivery windows, limited on-site staging, and equipment that cannot simply be swapped out midday. Tight staging is also a liability factor, because material and equipment stored in close quarters beside occupied homes is what generates property damage claims.

Underlying all of it is property value and elevation. Sanibel and neighboring Captiva carry high-value coastal homes, much of it rebuilt or repaired since Ian to current elevation and coastal construction standards. Crews are working at height on structures worth a great deal, adjacent to other structures worth a great deal. Contractors carrying only the state licensing minimum are frequently underinsured against the properties they are standing on, and excess or umbrella coverage is worth serious consideration for island work.
