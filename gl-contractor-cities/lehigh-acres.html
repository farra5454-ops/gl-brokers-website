Subcontracting at Volume: The Lehigh Acres Reality

Lehigh Acres is different from every other community in our service area in a structural way: it is unincorporated. There is no municipal government, no city building department, and no city permitting office. Everything runs through Lee County’s development services, and contractors used to dealing with a city hall sometimes lose time learning that. It also means the community has no local business licensing layer of its own — state licensure and county permitting are the whole picture.

What defines the market is volume. Lehigh Acres was platted in the 1950s into an enormous grid of small residential lots, and that inventory has fueled sustained single-family construction for years. The work is organized around builders who run many homes simultaneously and staff them with trade crews moving from lot to lot. Framing, concrete, roofing, stucco, and electrical subs here are working at a pace and a margin that leaves very little room for an uninsured loss.

In that structure, the certificate of insurance functions as the license to work. Builders manage their own risk by requiring every subcontractor to carry general liability naming the builder and the lot owner as additional insureds, and by verifying it before a crew gets scheduled. A lapsed certificate does not generate a warning here — it generates a gap in your schedule, because the builder simply assigns the work to a crew whose paperwork is current.

The scattered-lot pattern creates a specific and underappreciated exposure. Because Lehigh’s vacant lots are interspersed among occupied homes rather than grouped into fresh subdivisions, site prep and land clearing crews are almost always working immediately beside someone’s residence. Equipment damage to fences, driveways, and landscaping, debris blown onto neighboring property, and struck utility lines are routine third-party claims — and the neighbor filing them is not your customer and has no contract with you.
