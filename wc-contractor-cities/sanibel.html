Causeway Commutes and Elevated Work on Sanibel

Sanibel is a barrier island connected to the mainland by a single causeway, which Hurricane Ian severed in September 2022 before it was rebuilt and reopened. Nearly every contractor working here lives and stages on the mainland, so the workday begins and ends with a causeway crossing. That raises a workers’ compensation question employers ask about often and understand incompletely: whether travel time is covered.

The general Florida principle is that ordinary commuting to and from a fixed workplace is not covered, while travel between jobsites during the workday, or travel undertaken as part of the job duties, typically is. For Sanibel work the distinction gets practical fast. A crew driving from home across the causeway to a single island jobsite is generally commuting. A crew that reports to a mainland yard, loads materials, and then travels to the island as part of the day’s work is in a different position. Because the answer depends on the specific arrangement, it is worth structuring deliberately rather than discovering after an accident.

The construction exposure itself is genuinely elevated, in both senses. Sanibel’s land use plan imposes building standards stricter than the surrounding county, and much of the current work involves building or rebuilding to coastal elevation requirements. That means crews spending extended periods working at height on elevated structures. Falls are the most severe claim category in Florida construction — they produce the longest disability durations and the largest claim values — and this island’s building requirements concentrate that exposure.

Tree and vegetation work is the other high-rate category here. Sanibel protects its native canopy through ordinances stricter than neighboring jurisdictions, and storm recovery has kept removal and restoration crews working steadily. Tree work carries among the highest workers’ compensation rates in Florida for good reason: chainsaw injuries, falls from height, and struck-by incidents are frequent and severe. Add limited staging space, which forces more manual material handling in tight quarters, and lifting and handling injuries become a meaningful secondary exposure across all island trades.
