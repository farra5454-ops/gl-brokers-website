Roofing Contractors: Workers Comp Insurance in Florida

Few Florida trades carry roofing's physical risk — crews work at height in extreme heat, with storm demand nearly year-round. Workers' compensation keeps a crew protected and a business legally able to operate.

Quick Answer

In Florida, a roofing company doesn't need a big crew to trigger the workers' comp requirement, one employee is enough. Roofing work is classified under Class Code 5551 at a rate of $6.752 per $100 of payroll. What NCCI Classification and Rate Cover Roofing Work in Florida?

NCCI Class Code 5551 (“Roofing — All Kinds & Drivers”) is the classification that applies to roofing work in Florida, covering flat, sloped, and built-up roofing along with the driving exposure tied to hauling materials. As of January 1, 2026, its advisory rate sits at $6.752 per $100 of payroll. Staff who work behind a desk rather than on the roof, project managers and superintendents, are usually coded instead under 5606 (“Contracting — Executive Supervisor”), a much lighter rate of $0.85 per $100. Premium is calculated by multiplying each classification's payroll by its rate, so $100,000 in payroll under Code 5551 works out to a $6,752 base premium before any experience-mod adjustment. Keeping field and office payroll properly separated is one of the easiest ways to avoid overpaying.

Is Workers' Comp Legally Required for Roofers in Florida?

It is. Florida Statute 440 requires every contractor and subcontractor doing construction work, public or private, to carry workers' compensation for their employees. Construction trades don't get the small-business exemption other industries enjoy, so a roofing company with just one employee is already required to have coverage. The Florida Department of Financial Services (DFS) enforces this, and the Construction Industry Licensing Board (CILB) will hold up license issuance or renewal without proof of coverage or an approved exemption. Officers and LLC members can apply for an exemption under Statutes 440.04 and 440.05, though it only counts once properly filed and approved, not just claimed. The fallout for skipping coverage is steep: stop-work orders, fines, license complications, and the contractor personally covering an injured worker's medical costs and lost income.

What Coverage Mistakes Do Roofing Contractors in Florida Commonly Make?

The same handful of gaps trip up Florida roofing contractors over and over. Misclassifying employees as independent contractors tops the list, it might look like a savings until a denied injury claim leaves the owner facing back premiums, fines, and legal exposure all at once. Owner exemptions are another recurring problem: Statute 440 permits them for officers and LLC members, but only while the filing stays current, and lapsed exemptions during hurricane repair crunches have left more than one owner personally exposed. Not collecting certificates of insurance from subcontractors is equally risky, an uninsured sub's injured crew member becomes the general contractor's financial burden. And plenty of policies have a blind spot between workers' comp and general liability, leaving property-damage or third-party injury claims stuck disputing which policy should actually respond.

What Are the Biggest Safety Risks Roofing Crews Face in Florida?

Claims data backs up what anyone in the trade already knows, roofing is one of Florida's most dangerous jobs. Falls off ladders and roof edges, heat exhaustion during peak summer, and power-tool accidents account for most incidents, and when they occur they're rarely minor: broken bones, concussions, and medical bills that escalate fast.

What Benefits Does Workers' Comp Provide for an Injured Roofer?

When a roofer gets hurt on the job, workers' comp covers medical care, rehabilitation, and a share of the wages lost while they're off work. If the unthinkable happens and an accident proves fatal, dependents receive death benefits, so the family isn't left financially stranded on top of the loss.

What Factors Affect Workers' Comp Premiums for Florida Roofers?

A few factors combine to set the premium a Florida roofing contractor pays. Chief among them is the Experience Modification Rate (e-mod), which compares a contractor's claims history against similar businesses in the trade, below 1.0 lowers premiums, above 1.0 raises them. Payroll size carries just as much weight, since premium is payroll times the class code rate, and roofing's rate already runs high. A history of frequent or severe claims drags the e-mod upward over time, which is exactly why safety programs and disciplined claims handling tend to pay for themselves. Subcontractors without their own coverage are another factor to watch, their payroll often gets folded into the general contractor's exposure come audit time. And Florida's hurricane season adds a state-specific wrinkle: the repair surge that follows a storm brings more roofing work, more time pressure on crews, and typically more claims to go with it.
