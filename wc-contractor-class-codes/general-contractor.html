General Contractors: Workers Comp Insurance in Florida

General contracting in Florida means juggling large, fast-moving projects alongside a construction climate that swings from tropical heat to hurricane season. That combination of scale and risk is exactly why Workers' Compensation isn't a box to check here, it's part of how a well-run construction business protects its crews and its bottom line. Keeping your workforce covered is what keeps projects on schedule and your business financially sound in the Sunshine State.

Quick Answer

Florida Statute 440 requires any general contractor with even one worker on payroll to carry workers' comp. Purely supervisory roles are rated under Code 5606 at $0.85 per $100 of payroll, while hands-on carpentry work falls under Code 5403 ($4.36) or Code 5645 ($7.69) instead. Skipping coverage risks a stop-work order and fines running as high as $5,000 per day.

Which NCCI Class Codes and Rates Apply to Florida General Contractors?

The right NCCI class code for a Florida general contractor depends on what the crew actually does day to day. Supervisory roles like project managers and construction superintendents fall under Code 5606, carrying a 2026 advisory rate of $0.85 per $100 of payroll. Once a contractor's team gets hands-on with carpentry, different codes apply: Code 5403 (Carpentry NOC) at $4.36 per $100, or Code 5645 (Carpentry — Detached One or Two Family Dwellings) at $7.69 per $100, depending on the type of work performed. To calculate premium, multiply payroll assigned to each class code by its rate, divide by 100, then apply the company's Experience Modification Factor (E-Mod) to the total.

Do Florida General Contractors Have to Carry Workers' Comp?

Yes. Florida Statute 440 requires any construction industry employer with even one worker on payroll to carry workers' compensation, general contractors included. The Florida Construction Industry Licensing Board won't issue or renew a license without proof of coverage or a valid exemption on file. Skip this requirement and the state can hit you with a stop-work order, fines running as high as $5,000 per day, and in serious cases felony charges. Corporate officers are automatically covered unless they've filed for an exemption, and general contractors carry the added responsibility of confirming every subcontractor on the job has their own coverage in place.

What Are the Most Common Workers' Comp Mistakes General Contractors Make?

A few recurring mistakes show up again and again among Florida general contractors. Misclassifying employees as independent contractors to dodge premiums is the most common, and it tends to backfire hard when a claim gets filed. Skipping verification that subcontractors carry their own coverage is another, since an uninsured sub's injured worker becomes the general contractor's liability. Some owners exclude themselves from coverage to cut costs, which turns into an expensive gamble the moment they're the one injured on site. And a fair number of contractors don't fully grasp where workers' compensation ends and general liability begins, leaving gaps that surface at the worst possible time.

The Most Common On-the-Job Risks for Florida General Contractors

Falls from scaffolding and ladders, injuries tied to heavy equipment operation, and strain injuries from manual labor make up the bulk of workplace incidents for Florida general contractors. When they happen, the medical bills and lost income add up fast, which is exactly the gap comprehensive coverage is meant to close.

What Does Workers' Comp Actually Cover for General Contractors?

For an injured employee, workers' comp covers medical treatment, rehab, and a portion of lost wages while they recover. In the event of a workplace fatality, it extends death benefits to dependents. Most policies also bundle in employer's liability coverage, which can help cover legal defense costs if an injured worker takes the employer to court.

What Determines Workers' Comp Costs for Florida General Contractors?

Payroll size is the single biggest driver of premium cost, since more employees means more exposure and a bigger base to calculate against. The NCCI class codes assigned to the work matter just as much, riskier trades like carpentry carry meaningfully higher rates than supervisory roles. Claims history feeds directly into the Experience Modification Factor, so a track record of frequent or severe claims pushes costs up over time. Bringing on uninsured subcontractors adds to the exposure too, since their payroll can end up imputed to the general contractor. And Florida's hurricane season adds its own pressure, more storm-driven repair work tends to mean more claims and higher rates industry-wide.
