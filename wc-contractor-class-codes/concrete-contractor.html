Concrete Contractors: Workers Comp Insurance in Florida

Florida's construction market is growing quickly, and between rapid development and the state's unpredictable climate, concrete contractors are looking at real opportunity alongside real exposure. Concrete work is physically demanding on its own, and Florida's hurricane season and strict building codes add another layer of complexity, which is why solid insurance coverage isn't a nice-to-have. Workers' Compensation is a legal requirement, but more importantly, it's how you protect the people who make your business run.

Quick Answer

Concrete contractors in Florida need workers' comp coverage starting at just 1 employee. The primary NCCI-rated class code carries an advisory rate of $5.18 per $100 of payroll. Going without coverage risks a $1,000/day stop-work order and 2× unpaid premiums.

Which Class Code and Rate Apply to Concrete Contractors in Florida?

Concrete work in Florida spans several different NCCI class codes, each tied to a different type of job. Class code 5213, “Concrete Construction NOC,” is the broadest, covering general concrete work on commercial and industrial structures, including foundations, slabs, walls, and elevated structures, at a current Florida advisory rate of $5.18 per $100 of payroll, effective January 1, 2026. Ground-level work like residential and commercial slabs, driveways, and sidewalks typically falls under code 5215, “Concrete Construction — Floors, Driveways,” rated at $5.12 per $100 of payroll in Florida. Flatwork specifically, meaning sidewalks, curbs, and gutters, is classified under NCCI code 5221, “Concrete or Cement Work — Sidewalks,” at a Florida advisory rate of $3.87 per $100 of payroll. There's also code 5222, “Concrete Construction in Connection with Bridges or Culverts,” for concrete work tied to bridges and culverts specifically. However the work is classified, the premium comes from multiplying payroll for each classification by its NCCI advisory rate, then layering in adjustments like the experience modification rate (EMR) and other underwriting factors.

Do Concrete Contractors in Florida Need Workers' Comp?

Florida Statute 440 is the law that governs workers' compensation for concrete contractors, and it requires coverage for construction businesses with as little as one employee. That's a tighter threshold than most other industries face, reflecting how seriously the state treats worker protection in construction. Skip it, and the penalties are harsh: stop-work orders and fines that often run double the unpaid premium. Florida's contractor licensing boards also won't issue or renew a license without proof of workers' compensation or a valid exemption. Concrete contractors also carry responsibility for their subcontractors here: if a sub doesn't have proper coverage, the contractor above them can be on the hook for that sub's injuries. Corporate officers do have an exemption path, but it generally requires holding at least 10% ownership in the company.

Common Coverage Mistakes Florida Concrete Contractors Make

A few coverage mistakes tend to repeat themselves among Florida concrete contractors. Misclassifying employees as independent contractors is one of the biggest, and it's costly: fines can run $2,500 to $5,000 per misclassified worker, on top of additional premium payments and penalties from the Florida Department of Financial Services. Owner exclusions cause problems too. Corporate officers who hold at least 10% ownership can elect to be exempt, but filing that exemption incorrectly, or not understanding what it actually covers, can leave an owner without protection or create compliance headaches. Poor tracking of subcontractor certificates of insurance is another common gap. Contractors are on the hook for confirming their subcontractors carry required workers' compensation coverage, and if they don't, the primary contractor can end up liable for that sub's injuries. Finally, some contractors don't realize there's a gap between what Workers' Compensation covers and what General Liability covers. Workers' Comp handles employee injuries, but it doesn't touch third-party bodily injury or property damage, that's General Liability's job. Knowing where one policy ends and the other begins is essential to avoid being uninsured where it matters.

Common Workplace Risks for Florida Concrete Contractors

Concrete crews in Florida deal with a specific set of hazards on a regular basis. Falls from scaffolding or elevated forms are common and often result in severe fractures or head trauma. Being struck by heavy machinery, falling concrete slabs, or rebar is another frequent scenario, and it can lead to crushing injuries or amputations.

What Does Workers' Comp Cover for Concrete Contractors?

For concrete contractors in Florida, workers' compensation covers medical treatment, lost wages, and rehabilitation for employees injured on the job, regardless of who was at fault. In the rare case of a fatality, it also provides death benefits to dependents, offering financial support during an incredibly difficult time.

What Drives the Cost of Workers' Comp for Florida Concrete Contractors?

A handful of factors combine to determine what concrete contractors in Florida actually pay for workers' comp. The experience modification rate (EMR) matters most: a favorable EMR, meaning below 1.0, signals a strong safety record and can bring premiums down, while a higher EMR points to a rougher claims history and pushes costs up. Payroll size feeds directly into the premium too, since rates apply per $100 of payroll, which makes getting the payroll classification right essential. Claims history and EMR are closely linked, so frequent or severe claims will raise future premiums. How a contractor handles subcontractors matters as well; if a sub doesn't carry their own workers' compensation, the primary contractor can end up responsible for that coverage, which raises their own premium. Florida's hurricane exposure isn't a direct rating factor, but it can influence costs indirectly by ramping up construction activity and, with it, the potential for claims. And risk factors specific to concrete work, like heavy equipment, chemical exposure, and elevated work, are already baked into the base rates for the relevant NCCI class codes. Strong safety programs and active risk management go a long way toward keeping these cost drivers in check.
