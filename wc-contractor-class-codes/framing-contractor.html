Framing Contractors: Workers Comp Insurance in Florida

Working at height and handling heavy materials carries real risk, and Florida's climate and building pace add to it. Workers' Compensation isn't optional for framing contractors: it's a legal requirement and a core protection for your crew.

Quick Answer

In Florida, workers' comp coverage is required for framing contractors once they have just 1 employee. The primary class code is Class Code 5645, with an advisory rate of $7.69 per $100 of payroll. Skipping coverage carries steep penalties: a $1,000/day stop-work order and 2× unpaid premiums.

Which Class Code and Rate Apply to Framing Contractors in Florida?

Florida framing contractors can fall under a few different NCCI class codes, each carrying its own rate and scope of coverage. NCCI Class Code 5645, “Carpentry — Detached Dwellings,” applies to residential framers and rough carpenters building single-family homes, and carries a current Florida advisory rate of $7.69 per $100 of payroll as of January 1, 2026. Carpentry work that doesn't fit a more specific classification, including framing across a range of construction settings, falls under NCCI Class Code 5403, “Carpentry NOC,” rated at $4.36 per $100 of payroll in Florida. NCCI Class Code 5651, “Carpentry — Dwellings 3 Stories or Less,” is the third relevant code, covering framing and carpentry on multi-family residential buildings up to three stories, at a Florida advisory rate of $4.89 per $100 of payroll. In each case, the premium is calculated by multiplying payroll by the class code rate, then adjusting with the experience modification rate (EMR), so the final cost reflects both the type of carpentry work performed and the scale of the operation.

Do Florida Framing Contractors Need Workers' Comp?

Florida workers' compensation requirements are set out primarily in Florida Statute 440, which mandates coverage for the large majority of employers. Construction industry employers, framing contractors included, must carry coverage once they have even one employee, a lower threshold than the four-or-more-employee rule that applies outside construction. Compliance is overseen by the Florida Department of Financial Services, Division of Workers' Compensation, and the consequences of skipping coverage are steep: stop-work orders, substantial fines, and in some cases criminal exposure. Contractors also need to confirm that every subcontractor they use is carrying their own workers' compensation coverage. If a sub doesn't have it, the contractor above them can end up responsible for that sub's employees if someone gets hurt. Florida doesn't impose workers' comp-specific licensing requirements on top of general contractor licensing, but staying aligned with Statute 440 is essential to avoid legal trouble and protect the people doing the work.

Common Coverage Mistakes Florida Framing Contractors Make

A handful of coverage mistakes show up repeatedly among Florida framing contractors. Misclassifying employees as independent contractors is one of the most common: Florida's rules on this are strict, and getting it wrong can mean fines of $2,500 to $5,000 per misclassified worker, plus additional premiums and back wages. Owner exclusions trip people up too. Business owners can often elect to be exempt, but only if they meet specific ownership requirements (at least 10% ownership, for example), and improperly excluding an owner can leave that person with no coverage at all. Many contractors also fail to verify subcontractors' certificates of insurance. Under Florida Statute 440, if a subcontractor doesn't have their own workers' compensation policy or a valid exemption, responsibility for that sub's employees shifts to the primary contractor, an unwelcome surprise if it isn't caught in advance. And some contractors leave gaps between their workers' compensation and general liability policies, which undermines the protection either policy is supposed to provide. Closing those gaps matters as much as having the coverage in the first place.

Common Risks for Florida Framing Contractors

Framing work in Florida comes with a distinct set of hazards: falls from elevated framing, injuries from heavy lumber or power tools, and strains from repetitive lifting are among the most frequent. When these incidents happen, they tend to be serious, with fractures, concussions, and back injuries that require significant medical treatment being common outcomes.

What Workers' Comp Covers for Framers

For framing contractors in Florida, workers' compensation pays for medical treatment, lost wages, and rehabilitation for employees hurt on the job. In the event of a fatality, it also provides death benefits to dependents, offering crucial financial support at an incredibly difficult time.

What Drives the Cost of Workers' Comp for Florida Framing Contractors?

A number of factors shape what framing contractors in Florida pay for workers' comp. The experience modification rate (EMR) matters most; it's a multiplier that adjusts your premium based on how your claims history compares to similar businesses in your industry. A higher EMR means a rougher claims history and a bigger premium, while a lower EMR can mean meaningful savings. Payroll size matters directly too, since premiums are calculated per $100 of payroll, so larger crews mean higher costs by default. Claims history feeds straight into the EMR, so injuries on the job, especially frequent or severe ones, push costs up over time. How a contractor uses subcontractors matters as well: if they don't carry their own workers' compensation, that liability, and the added premium, can fall back on the primary contractor. Florida's hurricane exposure plays an indirect role too. It isn't a direct factor in premium calculation, but severe weather can make job sites more hazardous, which can lead to more claims and, eventually, a higher EMR. And framing-specific risks, like elevated work and heavy tool use, are already built into the base rates for the relevant class codes.

The Experience Modification Rate (EMR) is a multiplier applied to your workers' comp premium based on how your claims history compares to similar businesses of your size and type. Framing contractors are especially exposed here, since a single serious fall claim can push the EMR above 1.0 for three years and drive premiums up significantly. It's also worth knowing that many general contractors require framing subs to carry an EMR below 1.0 before they'll even consider them for a bid.
