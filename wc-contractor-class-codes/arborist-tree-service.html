Arborists & Tree Service Companies: Workers Comp Insurance in Florida

Tree work means climbing into the canopy, running a chainsaw at height, and feeding a chipper, often during hurricane season when storm calls come fast and on-site risk climbs. Workers' Compensation is what keeps a storm-response job from becoming a financial disaster.

Quick Answer

Tree service is treated as a non-construction trade under Florida law, so coverage becomes mandatory once a company has four or more employees, full-time or part-time combined, rather than the one-employee threshold construction trades face. Climbing and aerial work is rated under NCCI Class Code 0106 at $6.089 per $100 of payroll (effective January 1, 2026, minimum premium $769); ground-level landscape work falls under the lower-rated Code 0042 at $4.144 instead.

Which NCCI Class Code and Rate Applies to Florida Tree Service Companies?

Tree service crews in Florida are rated under NCCI Class Code 0106, “Tree Pruning, Spraying, Repairing — All Operations & Drivers.” It covers climbing, cutting, pruning, spraying, and removal work performed with hand tools, chainsaws, or mechanical equipment, whether crews are working from the ground, a ladder, or an aerial bucket, and it extends to the drivers who support that work. The Florida advisory rate for Code 0106 is $6.089 per $100 of payroll, effective January 1, 2026, with a minimum premium of $769. That's meaningfully higher than Code 0042, “Landscape Gardening & Drivers,” which covers ground-level lawn and garden work at a Florida advisory rate of $4.144 per $100 of payroll, because climbing and aerial work carry more injury risk than mowing and planting. Premium is calculated by dividing payroll by 100, multiplying by the class code rate, then applying the company's experience modification factor (EMR) to the result.

Do Florida Tree Service Companies Have to Carry Workers' Comp?

Tree service and landscaping work is generally treated as a non-construction industry under Florida law rather than construction, based on how Florida courts have drawn that line in tree-removal cases. That distinction matters because non-construction employers in Florida only need workers' compensation once they have four or more employees, full-time or part-time combined, rather than the one-employee threshold that applies to building trades. Work that's tied directly to a construction or land-development project can shift how it's classified, so it's worth confirming with an insurance advisor if your crew regularly works construction sites. Florida doesn't require a specific statewide contractor license for tree trimming or removal the way it does for trades like roofing or electrical, but many counties and cities require a local tree-removal permit, and proof of workers' comp is often part of that process. Tree service companies that bring in subcontractors, especially extra storm-response crews during hurricane season, are also responsible for confirming those subs carry their own coverage.

What Are the Most Common Workers' Comp Mistakes Tree Service Companies Make?

The mistake that shows up most often is misclassifying climbers and aerial crews under the lower-rated Code 0042 instead of Code 0106 to shave down premium, which is one of the fastest ways to trigger a painful audit bill once a carrier reviews the actual work being performed. Under-reporting payroll during storm season is another common gap, since hurricane cleanup work often means overtime, temporary labor, and subcontracted crews that need to be reported as they happen rather than caught at year-end audit. Owner exclusions cause trouble too, particularly when an officer files an exemption without understanding that it only covers them personally. Skipping certificate-of-insurance checks on subcontracted storm crews is a significant risk, since an uninsured sub's injury becomes the primary company's liability. And some tree service owners don't realize where workers' comp stops and general liability starts, workers' comp covers their own injured crew, not damage to a customer's roof or fence from a falling limb.

The Most Common On-the-Job Risks for Florida Tree Service Companies

Falls from height are the biggest risk in this trade, since climbers routinely work 50 feet or higher using ropes, harnesses, and saddles, and a fall from that height can mean broken bones, spinal injuries, or worse. Chainsaw injuries are close behind, kickback, a slipped grip, or a moment of lost focus can cause deep lacerations or amputations. Wood chippers are just as dangerous, giving almost no margin for error if a limb or a piece of clothing gets pulled in. Add in struck-by injuries from falling branches or rolling logs, and the risk of electrocution when work happens near power lines, and it's clear why this trade carries one of the higher-rated class codes. Storm cleanup work raises the stakes further, since unstable trees and debris-covered job sites make already dangerous work even riskier.

What Does Workers' Comp Actually Cover for Tree Service Employees?

For an injured crew member, workers' comp covers medical treatment, rehabilitation, and a portion of lost wages while they recover. If a workplace fatality occurs, which is a real risk given the height and equipment involved in this trade, it extends death benefits to dependents. Most policies also bundle in employer's liability coverage, which helps cover legal defense costs if an injured worker pursues a claim against the employer directly.

What Determines Workers' Comp Costs for Florida Tree Service Companies?

Payroll size is the biggest driver of premium, since more crew on the books means more exposure to rate against. Getting the class code right matters just as much, since climbing and aerial work under Code 0106 costs noticeably more to insure than ground-level work under Code 0042. Claims history feeds directly into the EMR, so a track record of falls, chainsaw injuries, or chipper accidents pushes costs up for years afterward. The amount of aerial and elevated work a company takes on is its own risk factor, since crews spending more time in the canopy carry more exposure than those doing mostly ground-based pruning. Florida's hurricane season adds its own pressure too, since storm-response work means more overtime, more temporary labor, and historically more claims industry-wide. And bringing on uninsured subcontractors for overflow storm work adds exposure that can end up imputed back to the primary company.
