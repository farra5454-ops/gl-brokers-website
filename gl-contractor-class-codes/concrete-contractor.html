Concrete Contractors: General Liability Insurance in Florida

Concrete sits at the center of Florida's construction growth, and of its risks. Hurricane season complicates curing, and constant infrastructure demand keeps crews under pressure. General Liability softens the blow when a third-party claim lands.

Quick Answer

The Construction Industry Licensing Board (CILB) requires Florida concrete contractors to carry General Liability coverage. The real-world standard runs $1,000,000 per occurrence / $2,000,000 aggregate — nearly every GC, property manager, and commercial client will ask for that limit. The DBPR licensing floor of $300K won't be enough to actually land work. Completed operations coverage is required under Florida Statute 627.441.

How Is General Liability Classified for Florida Concrete Contractors?

Getting the classification right matters a great deal for General Liability (GL) insurance on a Florida concrete contractor's policy, even though NCCI codes exist primarily for Workers' Compensation. For GL specifically, the relevant classification is usually ISO GL Code 91560, covering 'Concrete Construction' broadly, from foundations to flatwork. GL doesn't come with published advisory rates the way NCCI does for Workers' Comp. Instead, premium gets built from factors like gross sales or payroll, so a concrete contractor with $500,000 in annual gross sales would have that figure multiplied by a specific rate per $1,000 of sales to form the base premium. NCCI codes like 5213 (Concrete Construction — foundations) and 5221 (Concrete or Cement Work — Floors, Driveways, Yards, or Sidewalks & Drivers) exist for Workers' Comp, but they still help define the scope of work and risk that shapes GL underwriting. NCCI 5221's Florida advisory rate runs $3.87 per $100 of payroll, effective January 1, 2025. These classifications, taken together, let insurers assess the risks inherent to concrete work, structural integrity issues, property damage, and bodily injury exposure, that feed directly into the GL premium. Getting the classification right is what keeps risk assessment accurate and coverage appropriate for concrete contractors operating in Florida.

What Are Florida's GL Requirements for Concrete Contractors?

Chapter 627 of the Florida Statutes governs insurance rates and contracts broadly, including General Liability for concrete contractors, but it doesn't universally mandate GL coverage for every contractor the way Workers' Compensation is mandated. What actually drives the requirement in practice is licensing: the Florida Construction Industry Licensing Board (CILB) requires licensed general and building contractors to maintain minimum liability insurance, typically $300,000 for bodily injury and $50,000 for property damage. Concrete contractors may fall under a few different license types, but demonstrating adequate GL coverage is usually a prerequisite for licensure and project eligibility either way. Falling short of these licensing board requirements brings real consequences, including fines, license suspension, or revocation, as laid out in Florida Statute 489.147. On top of the state-level rules, many project owners and general contractors require concrete subcontractors to carry specific GL limits contractually, which makes it de facto mandatory for securing work in Florida regardless of the licensing floor. Falling short here can mean serious legal and financial repercussions, and it can affect a contractor's ability to operate legally and competitively.

Common GL Coverage Mistakes Florida Concrete Contractors Make

A handful of mistakes show up again and again among Florida concrete contractors managing their General Liability coverage. Assuming the policy covers everything is one of the most common, and it creates a real gap between General Liability and Workers' Compensation, since GL covers third-party bodily injury and property damage but not employee injuries, which fall under Workers' Comp instead. Failing to properly vet subcontractors and require certificates of insurance is another frequent mistake, since an uninsured or underinsured sub who causes damage or injury can end up tapping the primary contractor's GL policy, driving up premiums or leaving losses uncovered. Overlooking policy exclusions, like those for faulty workmanship or specific types of concrete damage, leaves contractors vulnerable in ways they don't discover until a claim gets denied. And underreporting gross sales or payroll to shave down premiums is a critical error that can trigger policy cancellation, denied claims, and severe penalties at audit time, leaving the business exposed to substantial financial risk right when it needs coverage most.

The Most Common Risks Florida Concrete Contractors Face

Accidental damage to client property during pours or demolition, a concrete truck splashing slurry onto nearby vehicles being a familiar example, is one of the most frequent risks Florida concrete contractors run into. Bodily injury claims come up too, when a third party trips over equipment or wet concrete on a job site.

What Does General Liability Actually Cover for Concrete Contractors?

At its core, General Liability for concrete contractors covers claims of bodily injury and property damage to third parties, legal defense costs, medical expenses for injured individuals, and repair or replacement costs for damaged property. It often extends to personal and advertising injury too, coverage that's easy to overlook until it's actually needed.

What Determines GL Insurance Costs for Florida Concrete Contractors?

A handful of factors combine to set what Florida concrete contractors actually pay for General Liability. Annual gross sales or payroll are the primary drivers, since they correlate directly with the scale of operations and the risk exposure that comes with it, more volume generally means a bigger premium. Claims history matters just as much, a track record of frequent or severe claims pushes rates up because of the perceived higher risk. The type of concrete work performed plays a role too, structural concrete work or projects with significant excavation tend to carry higher rates than simpler flatwork. How a contractor uses subcontractors is a key consideration as well, since inadequately insured subs can transfer their risk straight to the primary contractor's policy and drive up costs. And Florida's environmental factors, hurricane exposure especially, can influence GL premiums directly, with projects in coastal or high-wind zones facing higher rates because of the elevated potential for storm-related property damage claims.
