Arborists & Tree Service Companies: General Liability Insurance in Florida

Tree work means climbing into the canopy, running a chainsaw at height, and clearing storm damage feet from someone's roof, fence, or car. General Liability is what covers a tree service when the work damages property or injures a bystander.

Quick Answer

Florida doesn't license arborists or tree service companies the way it licenses roofers, electricians, or plumbers, so there's no state-mandated General Liability minimum tied to a contractor's license. In practice, though, the market standard still runs $1,000,000 per occurrence / $2,000,000 aggregate, since HOAs, property managers, commercial clients, and many county tree-removal permits won't accept anything less. General Liability for tree work falls under ISO Code 99777, and completed operations coverage is addressed under Florida Statute 627.441 the same as it is for any commercial general liability policy issued in the state.

How Is General Liability Classified for Florida Arborists?

Florida arborists and tree service companies are classified under ISO General Liability Code 99777, "Tree Pruning, Dusting, Spraying, Repairing, Trimming or Fumigating." The code is intentionally broad: it covers tree work performed from the ground, off a ladder, by climbing, or from a bucket truck, and it extends to incidental chipping, debris removal, stump grinding, storm cleanup, and the removal of a small number of trees. What determines the classification is the nature of the work itself, not whether a crew member ever leaves the ground, so a company doing purely ground-level pruning still falls under 99777 rather than a landscaping code. GL doesn't come with published advisory rates the way NCCI does for workers' comp; premium instead gets built from a contractor's payroll or gross receipts, with a rate applied per $1,000 of that exposure. Because climbing, chainsaws, and aerial work carry more injury and property-damage risk than ground-level lawn care, tree service typically prices higher than a landscaping company doing comparable revenue.

What Are Florida's GL Requirements for Arborists?

Unlike roofing, electrical, HVAC, framing, concrete, and plumbing work, tree trimming and removal isn't classified as a licensed construction trade under Florida law, so there's no Construction Industry Licensing Board (CILB) requirement mandating General Liability coverage the way there is for those trades. Florida only requires tree service companies to hold a standard local business tax receipt, and there's no statewide arborist licensing act the way some other states have. That said, coverage is functionally mandatory in most markets: many counties and municipalities require proof of General Liability insurance before issuing a tree-removal permit, particularly for protected or heritage trees, and HOAs, property managers, and commercial clients routinely require a $1,000,000/$2,000,000 certificate before they'll sign a contract. Florida Statute 627.441 governs how commercial general liability policies handle completed operations coverage, which matters for tree companies since a poorly pruned or improperly removed tree can cause damage well after the crew has left the property. Operating without adequate coverage doesn't carry the same license-suspension risk that CILB trades face, but it does mean losing out on most commercial and HOA work, and it leaves the business personally exposed to the full cost of any claim.

Common GL Coverage Mistakes Florida Arborists Make

Misclassifying tree work under a lower-rated landscaping code is one of the most common and costly mistakes tree service companies make in Florida. Some contractors assume they can split payroll or revenue between tree and landscape classifications the way they might for workers' comp, but ISO rules for General Liability don't work that way, the nature of the operation determines the code, and reporting ground-level pruning under a landscape classification is a frequent audit trigger that results in a large back-premium bill. Skipping completed operations coverage is another significant gap, since a tree that falls or a limb that fails weeks after a job wraps can still trigger a claim against the company that did the work. Failing to collect certificates of insurance from subcontracted storm crews, which many tree companies bring on during hurricane season, is a frequent oversight that can leave the primary contractor liable for an uninsured sub's damage or injury. And some owners overlook the gap between General Liability and Workers' Compensation, assuming GL will cover an injured crew member when it only covers third-party claims, not the company's own employees.

The Most Common Risks Florida Arborists Face

Property damage from falling limbs, trees, or equipment is one of the most frequent claims Florida tree service companies deal with, a poorly directed felling cut or an unexpected limb failure can total a roof, fence, pool enclosure, or parked vehicle in seconds. Bodily injury claims come up too, when a bystander, homeowner, or passing pedestrian gets hurt by falling debris, a dropped tool, or a piece of equipment on an active job site. Storm cleanup work raises the stakes further, since crews are often working around already-damaged structures, downed power lines, and unstable trees, all of which multiply the odds of a costly third-party claim.

What Does General Liability Actually Cover for Arborists?

At its core, General Liability for a tree service company covers third-party bodily injury and property damage claims, medical bills for an injured bystander, repair or replacement costs for damaged property, and legal defense fees if the business gets sued, even when the claim turns out to be unfounded. It extends to completed operations too, covering claims that surface after a job is finished, and often includes personal and advertising injury coverage, protection that's easy to forget about until it's actually needed.

What Determines GL Insurance Costs for Florida Arborists?

Tree service companies in Florida typically pay somewhere between $130 and $150 a month, roughly $1,500 to $1,800 a year, for a standard $1,000,000/$2,000,000 General Liability policy, though small operations doing lower-risk work can pay as little as $500 to $1,500 annually for GL alone. A handful of factors push that number up or down. Payroll and gross receipts are the foundation, since they scale directly with exposure. Claims history matters just as much, a single serious property-damage or injury claim can meaningfully raise renewal premiums for years. The type of work performed plays a real role too, crane-assisted removals and large-tree takedowns carry more risk than routine pruning and trimming, and price accordingly. How much a company relies on subcontracted crews, especially during storm season, affects cost as well, since an uninsured sub's exposure can transfer back to the primary contractor's policy. And Florida's hurricane season adds its own pressure, since storm cleanup work means more crews, more equipment, and more opportunities for a claim during the months when demand is highest.
