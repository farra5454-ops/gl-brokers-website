Electrical Contractors: General Liability Insurance in Florida

Florida's Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board sets minimum liability limits for your license, and most GCs ask for much more. Here is what the board requires, how general liability is classified and priced for electricians, and what belongs on your certificate.

Quick Answer

Under Florida Administrative Code Rule 61G6-5.008, licensed electrical contractors must carry at least $100,000 per person and $300,000 per occurrence in liability coverage, plus $500,000 in property damage coverage or an $800,000 combined single limit, with completed operations and products included. The certificate must name the DBPR Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board as certificate holder. In practice, many GCs, property managers, and commercial clients ask for $1,000,000 per occurrence and $2,000,000 aggregate, so the licensing minimum is rarely enough to win work.

How Is General Liability Classified for Florida Electrical Contractors?

General liability uses its own classification system, separate from the NCCI codes used for workers' comp. Electrical contractors are commonly classified under GL code 92478, electrical work within buildings, which covers installing, repairing, and maintaining electrical systems inside homes and commercial buildings. On the workers' comp side, the matching NCCI code is usually 5190, electrical wiring within buildings, priced on payroll.

General liability works differently. Most carriers price electrical contractors on gross receipts, applying a rate per $1,000 of annual revenue. That rate varies with the type of work, loss history, and the carrier, which is why an actual quote is worth more than a rough estimate.

What Are Florida's GL Requirements for Electrical Contractors?

The Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board, part of the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR), sets the minimum coverage in Rule 61G6-5.008: $100,000 per person and $300,000 per occurrence in liability, plus $500,000 in property damage or an $800,000 combined single limit, including completed operations and products.

The rule also sets what the certificate must show. It has to come from an insurance agency, list the policy number rather than a binder number, name the DBPR Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board as certificate holder, and give the board 30 days' notice of cancellation. The rule treats failing to keep the coverage in force as a violation, which can lead to discipline by the board.

On public construction projects insured under an owner-controlled insurance program (OCIP), Florida Statutes § 627.441 requires liability insurers to offer contractors coverage for current or completed operations beyond the period the program covers.

The Biggest Risks Florida Electrical Contractors Face

Property damage during installation or repair is one of the most common general liability claims for electricians. An electrical fire or damage to an existing structure is the classic example, and it can surface months after the job is finished. Third-party bodily injury is close behind, such as a homeowner or visitor hurt by faulty wiring or a hazard left on the job site.

Because claims can arrive long after the work is done, completed operations coverage matters. Under Florida Statutes § 95.11(3)(b), a construction claim can generally be brought up to 7 years after the certificate of occupancy or completion.

What Does General Liability Actually Cover for Electricians?

General liability for electrical contractors covers third-party bodily injury and property damage claims: medical bills for people who are hurt, repair or replacement of damaged property, and legal defense if your business is sued. It also covers personal and advertising injury claims, such as a dispute over something your business published.

It does not cover injuries to your own employees (that is workers' compensation), damage to your own tools and equipment, or the cost of redoing your own faulty work.

What Determines GL Insurance Costs for Florida Electrical Contractors?

Several factors shape what a Florida electrical contractor pays for general liability:

Revenue. Most carriers rate on gross receipts, so more revenue means more premium.
Type of work. Commercial, industrial, and high-voltage work usually rates higher than routine residential service.
Loss history. Carriers review your loss runs, and past claims raise the price.
Subcontractors. Money paid to subs who do not carry their own general liability can be added to your rated exposure.

Your experience modification rate (e-mod) is a workers' comp rating, so it affects your workers' comp premium, not your general liability premium.
